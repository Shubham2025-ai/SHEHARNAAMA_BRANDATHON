The Brass Hammers of Tambat Ali

Between the landmarks: A 30-minute journey into living craft, aroma and wada doorways.

Old city quarters like Pune’s Peths, Delhi’s Shahjahanabad, and Ahmedabad’s Pols hold layers of history that guidebooks reduce to battle dates and monuments. Sheharnaama invites you to experience what lies between: the rhythm of beating copper, the scent of stone-ground coriander, and the quiet dignity of teakwood thresholds.

The Wooden Threshold (Umbra)

“Look down at the floor before you look up at the arches. The threshold you just crossed was hewn from teakwood in 1884 — rubbed smooth by five generations of bare feet.”

You step off the busy thoroughfare into the quiet vestibule of a traditional wada in Kasba Peth. Typical tours point up toward stone bastions and military dates, but Sheharnaama invites you to pause at the umbra — the raised wooden sill at every threshold. In old Marathi homes, crossing the threshold was a deliberate ritual: stepping over it marked the transition from the dust and commerce of the bazaar into the sanctity and coolness of the private courtyard.

Look up into the inner chowk (central courtyard). Notice how the heavy teak pillars support double-cantilevered balconies without a single steel bolt. The air temperature here drops three degrees as the courtyard acts as a natural chimney, pulling cool air up through shaded wooden lattices.

🏛️
What to notice on your walk: Run your fingertips along the peacock carvings at the bracket joints. The wood is unvarnished CP teak, aged to a warm charcoal-brown that resists termites without chemicals.
08:15 – 16:30 · Chapter 2 Living Craft · People

The Rhythm of Beaten Copper (Tambat Ali)

“Tink... tink... tink. You hear the lane a full two minutes before you see it. This is mathar kam — the sound of copper being taught how to hold water.”

Turn into the lane where the cobblestones narrow. The noise of traffic fades, replaced by a rhythmic metallic percussion. You are in Tambat Ali, home to Pune’s coppersmith community since the 17th century. These are not souvenir stall vendors; they are hereditary master metalsmiths.

We stop beside Bapu Rao Tambat, 72, seated cross-legged on a wooden stool beside his iron anvil. He is shaping a traditional copper tambya (water vessel). With rapid, perfectly measured strokes of a curved hammer, he creates thousands of tiny dimples across the copper sheet. This isn't decorative ornament — the dimpling (mathar kam) hardens the malleable metal and increases its surface area, naturally keeping water chilled and sweet.

🔨
The Maker’s Story: Bapu Rao explains why factory-pressed vessels can never match hand-beaten copper: “A stamped machine pot is dead metal. A beaten pot remembers every blow. It survives four generations of weddings and kitchens.”
16:30 – 24:10 · Chapter 3 Aroma · Food Heritage

The Brass Samovar & The Stone Spice Depot

“Follow the aroma of roasted coriander seeds and stone-ground dagad phool. Heritage isn’t only what you see — it’s what you taste at nine in the morning.”

Two bends down a quiet alleyway, the metallic chimes give way to an intoxicating blend of toasted coriander, dry red chillies, and woodsmoke. We halt outside a 90-year-old spice depot with wooden drawers stacked high. Here, grandmother recipes for Goda Masala are ground on slow iron rollers that never heat the spices, preserving their volatile essential oils.

Next door is the corner Irani bakery stall. The brass tea samovar hums quietly, boiling strong Assam dust tea with crushed lemongrass and green cardamom. You are handed a warm glass of chai alongside oven-fresh Brun Pav — crisp on the exterior, steaming soft within, slathered with unpasteurized white butter.

☕
The Food Pitstop: Unlike generic tourist tours that rush past food stalls, Sheharnaama treats local culinary rituals as living architecture. Taste the subtle smokiness of the brass samovar infusion.
24:10 – 30:00 · Chapter 4 Everyday Life · Living Memory

What the Walls Remember (The Living Otla)

“A city lives in its in-between spaces — the doorstep gossip, the chalk rangoli drawn before dawn, and the memories shared over a folded morning paper.”

As the 30-minute sample journey concludes, we pause on the stone otla — the raised platform fronting old lane homes. Elders from three neighbouring families sit here reading the local morning newspaper in the mild morning sun. They wave to the postman, exchange news of whose grandson is returning from abroad, and debate the best season for mangoes.

This is what Sheharnaama is built for: not viewing an old city as an open-air museum frozen in stone, but understanding it as a living, breathing neighbourhood where memory, architecture, craft, and human kindness intertwine every single morning.
